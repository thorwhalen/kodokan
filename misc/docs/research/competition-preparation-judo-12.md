# Preparing a 12-year-old for judo competition in France — research

*2026-11-07 · scope: mental preparation for any competition, then combat sports, then judo, then 12-year-olds under the 2026-2027 French rules; technique categories; popular beliefs checked against evidence · requested for: a parent preparing a child for competition, and a narration script built on it*

## Summary and recommendation

Five findings carry most of the weight.

1. **Nerves are not the enemy.** Across sports, the intensity of pre-competition anxiety barely predicts performance (r about −0.10), while self-confidence predicts it a little better (r about 0.24) [1] [2]. In judo, studies disagree on whether winners or losers were more anxious before their bouts [3] [4]. So the goal for a child is to read the racing heart as "my body is getting ready" and to have something to do with it. Trying to feel calm is the wrong target. The evidence for reframing is modest: "I am excited" improves how people say they feel, and a 2025 replication found it did not improve how observers rated the performance [5] [6].
2. **Three mental skills have meta-analytic support: a fixed pre-performance routine, imagery added to physical practice, and short trained self-talk cues.** The routine has the largest effect, and it holds up best under pressure (g about 0.64–0.70 in experiments, not moderated by age) [7]; imagery is medium (d about 0.43) and works best alongside real practice [8]; self-talk is medium (ES about 0.48), with trials in 12–14-year-old swimmers and tennis players [9] [10]. None of the three has been tested in young judokas, and judo mental-training research in general is thin [11] [12].
3. **Breathing calms; it has not been shown to win.** Slow breathing with a long exhale and the "cyclic sigh" lower arousal and improve mood in adults [13] [14]. Use it to settle before the call to the mat. Do not sell it to the child as a performance trick.
4. **The child's environment matters more than any technique.** The best youth evidence concerns the adults: coaches trained to build a mastery climate (effort and learning over winning) lowered children's sport anxiety in controlled field studies [15], enjoyment loss is the leading reason children quit [16], and French federation texts say the same in their own words: for Minimes "l'animation, l'acquisition d'expériences, les contacts et les échanges priment sur le résultat sportif" [17] [18].
5. **French youth rules change what "the best technique" means.** A 12-year-old is a Minime if born in 2013–2014 and still a Benjamin if born in 2015 [19]. Minimes fight 3 minutes with no golden score; a drawn match goes to the referees, who look first at who threw more often and then at who attacked more [18]. Chokes, armlocks, neck pressure, the two-knee two-sleeve seoi-nage and head-first diving are out [18]. The rules reward a child who **attacks often, with clean standing throws, and follows a failed throw straight into a pin**. Leg throws (ashi-waza) are the most used group in cadets and at elite level [20] [21], and at elite level 69% of ground scores came straight after a failed standing attack [22].

**Recommendation.** Build preparation on a short, rehearsed routine (breath, cue word, one technical intention, bow), two or three process goals for the day ("attack first", "grip with both hands", "go to the ground after every throw"), five minutes of imagery and breathing on most days in the two weeks before, protected sleep across the whole week, and no weight cutting. Technically, a broad base of standing throws with one or two favourite attacks on both sides, plus the habit of chaining a failed throw into osaekomi, matches both the evidence and the French Minime rules. Popular beliefs that do not survive the evidence: power posing, lucky charms, "visualise the medal" as a substitute for rehearsing actions, and short growth-mindset talks.

The evidence chain is long, and the reader should keep it in mind: most of what is known comes from adults, from other sports, or from elite seniors, and has been applied to 12-year-old judokas by extrapolation. Each section below says which link of the chain it rests on.

## How the evidence is graded here

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Several meta-analyses or many randomised trials agree, and the effect survives checks for publication bias. |
| **Moderate** | One meta-analysis or a handful of good trials; plausible bias not ruled out. |
| **Weak** | Small or uncontrolled studies, or observational data only. |
| **Contradicted** | A well-powered replication or bias-corrected re-analysis failed to find the effect. |
| **Folk** | Widely said, never tested. |

The sport-psychology literature is made of many small studies, and the field's own re-analyses show that small positive studies get published more than null ones [27]. So read every "moderate" here as "probably real, probably smaller than reported".

## 1. Any competition: what mental preparation has evidence

### 1.1 Anxiety, confidence and how the athlete reads their body

The classic frame splits pre-competition anxiety into cognitive anxiety (worry), somatic anxiety (the racing heart, the butterflies) and self-confidence, measured with the CSAI-2 questionnaire. Pooling those studies, anxiety intensity correlates weakly with performance (cognitive anxiety r about −0.10) and self-confidence a little more strongly (r about 0.24), with stronger links in individual sports than in team sports [1] [2]. These are correlations: they do not show that raising confidence causes better results.

What separates performers more than the amount of anxiety is how they read it. Elite and non-elite swimmers reported similar anxiety intensity, but elites rated their symptoms as helpful more often [28]; a multimodal programme of goals, imagery and self-talk moved swimmers from reading their symptoms as harmful to reading them as helpful [29]. Critics argue that the "facilitative anxiety" idea rests on weak measurement [30], and none of this work was done with children.

Two interventions target the reading directly. Reappraisal ("I am excited") improved self-reported excitement and judged performance in non-sport tasks [5]; a 2025 replication reproduced the self-report effect and found no change in observer-rated performance [6]. In a pressurised darts task, a randomised arousal-reappraisal instruction changed cardiovascular responses, confidence and performance [31]. The related challenge-and-threat model says an athlete who sees the demands as within their resources (a challenge) performs better than one who sees them as exceeding their resources (a threat); lab trials in golfers support it [32] [33].

**For a child**: tell them the butterflies are normal, that they mean the body is getting ready to fight, and give them something to do with them (the routine in 1.4). Avoid "calm down", which asks for the impossible and implies the feeling is a problem. Grade: moderate for adults, untested in children.

### 1.2 Imagery (visualisation)

Imagery has the best-pooled evidence of the mental skills. A 2021 meta-analysis found a medium effect (d = 0.43, 95% CI 0.30–0.56), larger when imagery was added to physical practice than when used alone, and growing with the amount of imagery training [8]. The PETTLEP model (Physical, Environment, Task, Timing, Learning, Emotion, Perspective) recommends making imagery as close to the real action as possible: in the judogi, in real time, with the feelings of the day [34]. A PETTLEP trial ran with children [35], but the child and youth imagery evidence is mixed overall [36].

Two practical points follow. Imagery works as rehearsal of actions: the grip, the entry, the throw, the transition to the ground, the bow after a loss. Imagining the medal is a different thing and has no comparable evidence. The second point is dose: short, frequent sessions on top of real practice, never instead of it. Grade: moderate to strong in adults; weak and mixed in children.

### 1.3 Self-talk

A meta-analysis of 32 studies found a medium effect of self-talk on sport performance (ES = 0.48), larger when athletes were trained in their self-talk, with instructional cues ("both hands", "low hips") helping precise or new skills and motivational cues ("I can", "go") helping strength and effort tasks [9]. Youth trials exist: a 10-week programme in young swimmers improved competition performance, and motivational self-talk improved self-efficacy and performance in tennis players aged about 13 [10]. Talking to oneself in the second person or by one's own name ("you've got this") reduced rumination in lab stress tasks; it has not been tested on sport performance [37].

**For a child**: one to three short cue words, chosen with the coach, practised in training so they are automatic on the day. Grade: moderate, with some youth support.

### 1.4 Pre-performance routines

A routine is a fixed short sequence done before each performance. A 2021 meta-analysis (112 effect sizes, 15 sports) found g = 0.64 under low pressure and g = 0.70 under pressure in experimental designs, with no moderation by age, sex or skill level [7]. Of everything in this report, this has the best combination of effect size, resistance to pressure and plausibility for a child. A routine also replaces superstition: it gives the same feeling of control, and it is under the athlete's own control.

**For a judoka**, a natural routine already exists around the bow: tighten the belt, one slow breath with a long exhale, the cue word, one technical intention ("first grip"), bow, step forward. The point is to do it identically in every training randori and every match. Grade: moderate to strong.

### 1.5 Breathing

Breathing is the most recommended technique in the popular sources (section 6), and its evidence is weaker than its popularity. Heart-rate-variability biofeedback and slow breathing (around 6 breaths per minute) improved psychophysiological markers in small sport studies; reviews call the evidence limited (one review: 7 studies, 1 to 30 athletes each) [14] [38]. The most cited recent trial randomised adults to five minutes a day of cyclic sighing (two inhales through the nose, a long exhale through the mouth), box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation or mindfulness for a month: cyclic sighing improved mood and lowered breathing rate the most [13]. That is a mood trial in adults, not a sport trial, and no breathing technique has been tested for competition performance in children.

**For a child**: breathing is a good way to come down before the call to the mat and between matches, and to fall asleep the night before. Practise it daily so it works under stress. Two simple forms: the cyclic sigh, or a count of four in and six out. Grade: weak for performance, moderate for calming.

### 1.6 Goals

Goal setting has a small to moderate effect overall (g = 0.34 in the older meta-analysis) [39]; a 2022 meta-analysis found process goals (what I do) had the largest effects, though the reported size is large enough to suggest small studies [40]. For competition day, process goals are under the child's control, survive a loss, and fit what the French referees reward (section 4).

### 1.7 Attention under pressure

Skilled performers "choke" when pressure makes them watch their own movement step by step (explicit monitoring) [41]. The popular remedy, an external focus of attention (think about the effect, not the body), was supported by earlier meta-analyses, but a 2024 bias-corrected re-analysis found its effect close to zero (g = 0.01 for performance) [27]. The safe conclusion for the mat: on competition day, no new technical corrections and no long instructions. One intention per match. The coach's message between matte and hajime should be one or two ideas, because athletes retain only part of what is said before a judo bout [42].

### 1.8 Sleep and food

Most elite athletes sleep worse the night before an important event (64% in one survey, mostly from thoughts and nerves) [43], and in one study that disturbed night was not related to performance [44]. Sleep deprivation clearly harms power, speed and skill [45], but those studies mostly test far more sleep loss than one restless night. So: protect sleep across the whole week, and tell the child that one bad night before a tournament is normal and does not decide anything. That second message is itself useful, since worry about sleep is part of what keeps people awake.

### 1.9 What fails

- **Power posing**: a 200-person replication found it changed self-reported feelings of power and nothing else (no hormone or behaviour change) [46]. Grade: contradicted.
- **Lucky charms**: the 2010 "lucky ball" putting result failed two high-powered replications [47]. Grade: contradicted. A routine gives the same sense of control without the magic.
- **Short growth-mindset lessons**: in school settings, mindset interventions show weak effects that shrink further when bias is accounted for [48] [49]. Praising effort and learning remains good coaching (1.10); a one-off mindset talk is not a preparation method.

### 1.10 The adults around a child

For children, the strongest evidence is about the adults. Coaches trained in the Mastery Approach to Coaching (reward effort and improvement, treat mistakes as part of learning) had players aged about 11–12 who reported lower sport anxiety than players of untrained coaches [15]. Reviews of parental involvement find it shapes children's experience strongly but warn that "good versus bad parent" summaries oversimplify [50]. A systematic review of dropout in organised youth sport puts loss of enjoyment, low perceived competence and social pressure at the top [16]. Grade: moderate, and in children.

## 2. Combat sports

Combat sports share three features that change preparation: a direct opponent, repeated short high-intensity efforts, and weight categories.

**Anxiety and hormones.** In a French judo study, losers had higher cognitive anxiety and lower self-confidence before their first bout; cortisol rose before competition in everybody and did not separate winners from losers [3]. In an international judo competition, winners had higher anticipatory cortisol, and the record reports higher cognitive anxiety in winners too [4]. In karate, the anger level at which athletes performed best varied from low to high between individuals [51], which is the individual-zones idea: there is no single right level of activation. A grappling-coaching claim that most fighters perform best at "5 to 7 out of 10" is opinion [52]. **For a child**: help them notice what their good matches felt like (too flat, too wound up, just right), instead of telling them to calm down or to get fired up.

**Mental training.** A systematic review of mental training in combat sports found 21 studies, all with athletes aged 16 or over; mental training seemed to improve anxiety, mood, confidence and performance, from few high-quality studies [12]. The judo-specific review found little evidence on imagery and called for experiments [11]. No randomised trial of imagery, self-talk, routines or breathing in young judokas was found.

**Weight cutting.** Rapid weight loss is common in judo and starts young: Brazilian judokas reported starting at 12.6 years on average [53], and in a survey of 108 Israeli boys aged 12–17 coaches were the main influence for about two thirds [54]. Rapid loss affects physical and cognitive capacities [55], and two weeks of weight reduction worsened mood (more anger, fatigue and depression, less vigour) in adult judokas [56]. The French texts weigh children in underwear on the day of competition with a 100 g (boys) or 200 g (girls) tolerance [57]; a Brittany committee states it does not want young Minimes to diet [58]. **For a 12-year-old: no cutting.** Fight at natural weight; the category is a fact of the day, not a target.

**The physiology of a bout.** A judo contest alternates efforts of 20–30 seconds with pauses of about 10 seconds (ratio about 2:1 to 3:1), using all three energy systems, with grip fighting taking a large part of effort time [59]. After a bout, active recovery lowers lactate faster than rest but did not improve the next effort [60]; a judo-specific warm-up gave better judo-specific performance than no warm-up or a generic dynamic one [61]. The French rules give Minimes 6 minutes between matches (twice the match time) [17]: enough to sit, drink, breathe, and re-warm briefly with a few uchi-komi before the next call.

## 3. Judo

**What scores at the top.** In 1,769 senior World Tour fights (2017–2018), leg throws were the most used group (36–49% of throws depending on the team), then hand throws (27–29%), sacrifice throws (9–26%) and hip throws (6–10%); 89–93% of attacks were single attacks, only 2.6–4.7% combinations, and 68–70% were forward throws [21]. At London 2012, the seoi-nage family accounted for 14.8% of scored throws and groundwork for 17.7% of all scored actions [62]. At the 2013 Worlds, most scores came from direct attacks, forward throws, and opposite-stance (kenka-yotsu) situations [63].

**Groundwork follows a failed throw.** At the 2018 World Championships, 74% of groundwork scores were pins (osaekomi), and 69% of all groundwork scores came straight after a failed standing attack, mostly to the attacker [22]. That is the strongest data-backed habit in this report for a child under French rules, where chokes and armlocks are forbidden and only pins remain on the ground.

**Variety wins at the top.** Super-elite medallists used more different throws, in more directions, than other elite medallists, and variety correlated with wins and ippons [64]. This is correlational and about senior men, but it agrees with the motor-learning evidence that mixing similar skills in practice produces better long-term learning than drilling one at a time [24].

**Laterality.** Left-sided fighters were over-represented among winners in some adult samples but not others [65]. In 90 under-15 judokas, left-sided fighters were more effective and more likely to medal [66]. Attacking on the same side as the opponent's grip raised the odds of scoring in elite matches [67], and in adult beginners eight weeks of training only the non-dominant side flipped their lateral preference [68]. So a second side is trainable, and there are hints, not proof, that it pays at youth level.

**Rule changes.** Studies of the 2010–2022 rule changes measured scores and penalties (more penalties, fewer small scores), not which throws became more or less effective [69] [70]. The 2025 IJF rules brought back yuko and kept golden score [71]; French youth rules, below, go their own way.

**What elite French judokas say.** Teddy Riner describes accepting before each match that "you can win or lose" and wanting to be "an actor in your match and not a spectator", and credits a psychologist he began seeing as a teenager [72]. Clarisse Agbegnenou has said she was so stressed at Rio 2016 that she took no pleasure in it, and much calmer later [73]. These are testimony, and they agree with the evidence in 1.1: acceptance of the outcome, and attention on one's own actions.

## 4. Twelve-year-olds in France (season 2026–2027)

### 4.1 The rules that apply

| | Minime (born 2013–2014) | Benjamin (born 2015–2016) |
|---|---|---|
| A 12-year-old is… | in this category if born in 2014 (first year) or 2013 | in this category if born in 2015 |
| Match time | 3 min, no golden score; team events 2 min | 2 min, plus 1 min of ne-waza if tied (regional choice) |
| Rest between matches | 6 min | 4 min |
| Winner if no ippon | waza-ari lead, then yuko lead, then referees' decision | referees' decision |
| Referees' decision criteria | 1. who threw most often and/or held a pin under 5 s; 2. who attacked most; a shido does not automatically win | same |
| Forbidden | chokes, armlocks, neck pressure, cross grip, bear hug, fingers inside sleeve or trousers, seoi-nage or sode on two knees holding both sleeves, reverse seoi-nage | all Minime bans, plus sacrifice throws (sutemi), makikomi, direct attacks from two knees |
| Head | head touching the mat alone, on purpose, in attack or defence: hansoku-make (disqualification) | same |
| Back throws (new 2026–27) | matte if Uke goes above Tori's shoulder line and lands on upper shoulders or head | same |
| Penalties | first fault free, with an explanation; shido on repeat | same |
| Belt, licence | orange belt minimum for the Coupe de France; two licence seasons | yellow-orange minimum for the Critérium |
| National event | Coupe de France individuelle minimes (by regional selection) and team Coupe | none; departmental/regional Critérium |
| Weigh-in | day of competition, underwear, 100 g (boys) / 200 g (girls) tolerance; no weight surclassement | same |

Sources: birth years and weigh-in [19] [57]; match rules, bans and decision criteria [18]; events [17] [74]. Sutemi and makikomi are banned "up to Benjamins" in the federation's summary table, which implies they are allowed for Minimes; no text says so explicitly. Minime and Benjamin results earn no grade points, since competition points toward dan grades start at Cadet [75]. Regional circuits differ, so check the ligue and département rules for the specific tournament.

### 4.2 What the rules mean for preparation

- **Attacking is the decision criterion.** Without golden score, a close Minime match is decided on throws and attacks. A child who waits loses the decision even without being thrown. Process goal: "attack first, attack often".
- **Two-hand grips are the norm.** After a one-hand attack the judoka must return to a two-hand grip [18]. Grip fighting is short and simple at this level; cadets spend 38–59 s per match gripping [20].
- **On the ground, only pins.** With chokes and armlocks out, the ground game is osaekomi and turnovers into osaekomi, and even a pin under 5 s counts in the decision [18]. The "failed throw, straight into a pin" habit (section 3) is worth more here than at senior level.
- **The drop seoi-nage that many small children rely on is banned** when done on two knees with both sleeves held, and so is the reverse seoi-nage [18]. A child whose favourite throw is a two-knee drop needs a standing version before the season's competitions.
- **Big back throws are refereed for amplitude.** Ura-nage and o-soto-gaeshi-style throws that land Uke on the upper shoulders or head are stopped without a score [18].
- **Safety culture is built in**: first faults are free, the head-first rule is absolute, and a collective warm-up is compulsory at youth events [18]. French competition injury surveillance over 21 seasons found injuries in about 1% of fights, mostly sprains, with serious head and spine injuries rare [76].

### 4.3 Growth, maturation and the birth-month effect

At 11–14, biological maturation explains more of a judoka's strength and fitness than chronological age [77], and the maturity gap between two children of the same age can be large. The relative age effect exists in judo but is moderate: among cadet World Championship place-winners, those born early in the year were 1.72 times as likely as those born late in the year to be there, concentrated in boys and middle to heavy weights [78] [79]. For a parent this means two things. A child born late in the year, or maturing late, may lose to bigger or more mature opponents for reasons that say nothing about their judo. And early results predict adult success only loosely: cadet and junior world medallists had longer careers in some years and not in others [80], and Japan cancelled its national individual tournament for primary-school children in 2022, citing pressure and burnout [81].

### 4.4 Technique categories for a 12-year-old: what the data supports

No peer-reviewed study of technique frequencies in under-13 or under-15 competition was found. The closest data are cadets, 13–14-year-old girls, and 90 under-15 judokas [20] [82] [66]. Everything in the table extrapolates from those and from seniors.

| Category | What the data says | Fit with French Minime rules | Verdict for a 12-year-old |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ashi-waza (leg throws: o-uchi-gari, ko-uchi-gari, de-ashi-barai, o-soto-gari, uchi-mata) | Most-used group in cadets (median one per match) and at elite level; younger age groups used them more than seniors [20] [82] [21] | Fully allowed; they generate many attacks, which the decision rewards | **Core.** Two or three, with at least one forward and one backward direction |
| Te-waza (hand throws: seoi-nage, tai-otoshi, sukui-nage) | Seoi-nage family about 15% of senior scored throws; ippon-seoi-nage and sukui-nage frequent in European cadets [62] [83] | Standing seoi-nage allowed; two-knee two-sleeve and reverse versions banned | **Core**, in the standing form |
| Koshi-waza (hip throws: o-goshi, harai-goshi) | 6–10% of senior throws; rare in cadets [21] [20] | Allowed; back-throw amplitude rule does not apply | Useful learning base; less often a scoring weapon |
| Sutemi-waza (sacrifice: tomoe-nage, sumi-gaeshi) | 9–26% of senior throws, highest scoring rate among Rio champions (six athletes) [21] [84] | Banned for Benjamins; allowed for Minimes by inference | Optional for Minimes, with a coach; not a first weapon |
| Osaekomi-waza (pins) | 74% of elite ground scores; 69% of ground scores follow a failed throw [22] | The only scoring ground technique; a pin under 5 s counts in the decision | **Core**, especially the transition from a failed throw |
| Shime / kansetsu (chokes, armlocks) | 14% and 11% of elite ground scores [22] | Forbidden | Not for competition at this age |
| Combinations (renraku-waza) | Only 3–5% of senior attacks; no youth data [21] | Each attack counts toward the decision | Teach one simple combination to the favourite throw |
| Second side | Left-sided U15 fighters more effective in one study; trainable in adults [66] [68] | Neutral | Worth building now, while habits form |

Long-term development guidance from British Judo (coach education, not trials) puts boys of 12–14 in "sampling to specialising" years: a wide range of throws in several directions, introduction of sutemi-waza, continued work on a personal favourite throw (tokui-waza), and about 18 throws known across the groups [85]. That fits the data above: broad base, one or two favourites, both sides.

## 5. What people say, against what the evidence says

The popular sources (coaches, athletes, forums, parent guides, federation texts in English and French) converge on a small set of claims [86]. Judo-specific forum material was thin; most of the forum evidence is from Brazilian jiu-jitsu.

| What people say | Who says it | What the evidence says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Nerves are normal; they mean you care" | coaches, elite judokas, forums [87] [72] | Anxiety intensity predicts little; reading it as readiness is associated with better performance [1] [28] | **Supported** |
| "Tell yourself you're excited" | BJJ blogs [88] | Self-report effect replicates; performance effect does not [6] | **Partly supported**: helps how they feel |
| "Visualise yourself winning" | judo media, coaches [89] | Imagery of actions works (d about 0.43), best with physical practice [8]; no comparable evidence for outcome imagery | **Supported if it rehearses actions**, unsupported as medal imagery |
| "Breathe deeply / box breathing / physiological sigh" | most-repeated tip; wellness media | Calms and improves mood [13]; no performance trial | **Supported for calm**, unproven for winning |

| "Compete often; competition is a skill" | coaches, forums [87] | Plausible (practice under pressure) but not tested in youth judo; British Judo suggests a limited number of events per year at this age [85] | **Folk, plausible**, within limits |
| "Lucky charm / same socks" | popular media | Failed replication [47] | **Contradicted**; replace with a routine |

## 6. A preparation plan built on this

Each item is tagged with the grade of what it rests on.

**Two to three weeks before**
- Agree with the coach on two or three favourite attacks, one combination, and the "failed throw into a pin" habit. Practise them on both sides, mixed rather than in blocks. (moderate, extrapolated)
- Build the bow routine and use it in every randori. (moderate to strong)
- Five minutes on most days: two minutes of slow breathing, then three minutes of imagery of one's own match: the bow, the grip, the first attack, a throw that fails and the pin that follows, getting up after being thrown. (moderate for imagery, weak for breathing)
- Choose one to three cue words. (moderate)

**The last week**
- Lighter training in the last days; no new techniques. (folk, plausible)
- Normal bedtime all week. (moderate)
- Normal meals; no dieting for the weigh-in. (strong for avoiding harm)

**The night before and the morning**
- Pack the bag together (judogi, belt, licence and passeport sportif, water, familiar food). Knowing the plan for the day reduces uncertainty. (folk, plausible)
- If sleep does not come, a few minutes of breathing; one bad night does not decide anything. (moderate)

**At the venue**
- Arrive early; find the mats, the weigh-in, the warm-up area. (folk, plausible)
- Join the collective warm-up, then a short judo-specific warm-up before the first match. (moderate)
- Before each call: breathing, cue word, one intention, routine, bow. (moderate)
- Between matches (6 minutes for Minimes): sit, drink, breathe, a few uchi-komi before the next call; the coach gives one idea, not five. (moderate)

**After**
- Win or lose, the first sentence from a parent is about enjoying watching them. Debrief only if the child wants to, and later. (moderate for the climate; folk for the timing)
- The coach, not the parent, leads the technical review at the next training. (folk, plausible)

## Open questions

- No study of mental-skills training in judokas under 16 was found; the recommendations above extrapolate from other sports and older athletes.
- No technique-frequency data for under-13 or under-15 competition exist in the literature found; a club or league could produce them from video of its own tournaments. The kodokan repository's pose and throw-recognition tools are the beginning of such a pipeline.
- Whether sacrifice throws are explicitly allowed for Minimes, and the exact pin durations at this age, could not be confirmed in the 2026–2027 texts; ask the referee commission of the ligue.
- The figures from the French injury surveillance study were taken from a search summary and should be checked against the paper.
